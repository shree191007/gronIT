# ANTARCTIC NEOCLOUD
## An Ecology-Constrained Distributed Compute Network

**Ideathon Pitch Document | Concept Proposal**

**Thesis:** Build a distributed neocloud that couples subsea computing, ocean-aware infrastructure placement, adaptive ecological controls and waste-heat recovery into one optimised system.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence is increasing demand for compute infrastructure, while data centres face growing challenges involving electricity consumption, cooling, water use, grid capacity and lifecycle emissions.

We propose **Antarctic Neocloud**, a research-led concept for deploying modular, self-monitoring compute nodes in the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC) region. The system would use a floating platform with suspended subsea compute modules, closed-loop cooling, an ecological monitoring network and an optimisation engine that jointly evaluates compute performance and environmental impact.

Unlike conventional data-centre designs, the system treats environmental constraints as part of infrastructure orchestration. Ant colony optimisation (ACO) would explore candidate node placements and network configurations, while independent ecological constraints determine which configurations are permissible. The resulting designs would be evaluated using a multi-objective Pareto frontier.

Rejected heat would be assessed for useful recovery through low-temperature thermal processes, including membrane distillation. Hydrogen production would be considered as a separate electrolysis subsystem requiring its own electricity supply. Salt recovery would be evaluated only if its material balance, environmental impact and legal feasibility justify it.

Our goal is not to assume that placing a data centre underwater makes it sustainable. It is to develop a system that can quantify the trade-offs between delivered compute, ecological exposure, reliability, lifecycle emissions and total cost.

## 2. The Problem

### The infrastructure challenge

Modern AI infrastructure requires reliable compute, thermal management, power, networking and physical maintenance. Conventional terrestrial facilities face constraints related to energy availability, cooling infrastructure, land, water and environmental impacts across their lifecycle.

Subsea infrastructure offers an alternative environment for thermal management and remote deployment, but introduces its own challenges: corrosion, difficult repairs, network connectivity, mooring systems, marine exposure and decommissioning.

### The ecological challenge

Moving infrastructure offshore does not eliminate its environmental footprint. It changes where and how that footprint occurs.

Potential concerns include:

- Thermal discharge and local water-column changes.
- Acoustic disturbance from pumps, machinery and vessels.
- Chemical exposure from corrosion, coatings, coolants and process streams.
- Seabed disturbance from anchors, moorings and cables.
- Organism interactions with structures and seawater intakes.
- Brine discharge associated with water treatment and salt recovery.
- Cumulative effects from a network of installations.

The Southern Ocean is also important to global circulation, marine productivity and biogeochemical cycling. The Antarctic Circumpolar Current is not an unlimited environmental sink.

### The design gap

Existing research addresses subsea data centres, data-centre cooling, waste-heat recovery, marine environmental effects and multi-objective optimisation. Our proposed contribution is to integrate these disciplines into one constrained infrastructure-design framework.

## 3. Our Proposed Solution

Antarctic Neocloud consists of four interconnected systems.

### A. Modular subsea compute

Deploy sealed, independently recoverable compute modules suspended beneath a floating surface platform.

Each module would contain:

- GPU or other compute hardware.
- Closed-loop coolant circulation.
- Power conversion and electrical protection.
- Thermal, vibration, corrosion and leak monitoring.
- Remote fault isolation and recovery controls.

The platform would use a minimal-footprint mooring architecture, subject to structural and site-specific engineering. A single primary mooring could reduce the number of seabed interventions, but anchor placement, cable touchdown, drift and common-mode failure would still need to be addressed.

### B. Ocean-aware cooling

Use seawater as an external heat sink through isolated heat exchangers rather than directly exposing sensitive equipment to seawater.

The cooling architecture would be evaluated against:

- Chip temperatures and compute performance.
- Pumping and heat-exchanger energy consumption.
- Thermal plume dispersion.
- Marine exposure limits.
- Maintenance requirements and lifecycle costs.

The system would prioritise thermal efficiency while keeping discharge effects within independently established environmental limits.

### C. Ecological intelligence

Build a continuous monitoring layer using oceanographic sensors and environmental models.

Variables would include temperature, currents, dissolved oxygen, turbidity, acoustic exposure and relevant biological indicators.

The system would maintain baseline measurements, compare observations against reference locations, estimate environmental uncertainty and trigger operational responses when thresholds are approached.

### D. Thermal resource recovery

Recover heat from the compute cooling loop before final heat rejection.

The initial investigation would focus on processes compatible with the actual coolant temperatures, such as membrane distillation and other low-temperature thermal applications.

Hydrogen production would be evaluated separately. Waste heat can support some thermal processes, but water electrolysis requires electricity. Salt recovery would require concentration, crystallisation, purification and a safe strategy for residual brine.

Neither hydrogen nor salt revenue would be assumed in the baseline business case.

## 4. The Ant Colony Optimisation Engine

The optimisation engine is the proposed intelligence layer connecting the infrastructure and its environmental constraints.

ACO would explore candidate placements and network configurations using environmental, engineering and service data.

### Inputs

- Ocean-current and temperature fields.
- Bathymetry and seabed characteristics.
- Habitat and species-distribution information.
- Existing protected areas and legal restrictions.
- Fibre connectivity and estimated network latency.
- Power availability and cooling requirements.
- Mooring, deployment and maintenance costs.
- Uncertainty in environmental observations.

### Outputs

- Candidate node locations.
- Network topology.
- Cooling and discharge configurations.
- Workload-placement policies.
- Estimated service and ecological performance.
- Feasibility and uncertainty assessments.

### Ecological safeguards

ACO is a search method, not an ecological certification system. The optimiser would therefore operate under hard constraints that it cannot trade away for additional compute performance.

Examples include prohibited deployment areas, site-specific thermal exposure limits, acoustic limits, structural safety requirements and minimum environmental monitoring standards.

The algorithm would be benchmarked against simpler constrained optimisation and multi-objective evolutionary methods. Its value would be demonstrated through measurable improvements in the quality of feasible solutions.

## 5. The Complete Trade-Off Matrix

The venture's defining challenge is the interaction between service performance and environmental impact. Each design decision has operational benefits, ecological costs and economic consequences.

| Design decision | Service benefit | Ecological or operational cost | Evaluation metric |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compute density | Higher GPU capacity per platform | Concentrated thermal and power loads | Delivered GPU-hours vs thermal exposure |
| Cooling intensity | Better chip temperatures | Pumping demand and discharge effects | Cooling efficiency vs ecological exposure |
| Number of nodes | Greater capacity and redundancy | More materials, structures and maintenance | Available compute vs lifecycle footprint |
| Node placement | Improved connectivity and operating conditions | Habitat disturbance and uncertain environmental risk | Latency vs ecological risk |
| Mooring design | Stability and station-keeping | Anchor, cable and seabed impacts | Availability vs physical footprint |
| Network topology | Better bandwidth and fault tolerance | Additional cable installation and material use | Latency and throughput vs cable footprint |
| Power redundancy | Improved uptime | More equipment and embodied emissions | Availability vs lifecycle impact |
| Workload scheduling | Higher utilisation and throughput | Less flexibility during sensitive conditions | GPU utilisation vs ecological exposure |
| Environmental throttling | Limits exposure and prevents escalation | Lost capacity and potential SLA violations | Impact avoided vs compute lost |
| Heat recovery | Useful thermal output | Added equipment and operating complexity | Useful heat vs total system impact |
| Hydrogen production | Potential additional product revenue | Electricity demand and process hazards | Hydrogen output vs lifecycle emissions |
| Salt recovery | Potential material recovery | Brine, purification and residual-stream impacts | Product recovery vs discharge impact |
| Acoustic mitigation | Reduced noise exposure | Additional mass, cost and possible cooling penalties | Acoustic exposure vs service performance |
| Monitoring density | Earlier detection of environmental changes | Sensor energy, maintenance and data uncertainty | Detection capability vs cost |
| Maintenance frequency | Faster fault detection and recovery | Vessel emissions and physical disturbance | Recovery time vs lifecycle impact |
| Spare capacity | Greater resilience during shutdowns | Additional hardware and capital expenditure | Resilient capacity vs footprint |
| Decommissioning design | Safer retrieval and reduced abandonment risk | Upfront cost and recovery infrastructure | Recovery success vs lifecycle cost |

The matrix makes one principle explicit: the system must optimise the complete infrastructure lifecycle, not just the efficiency of the cooling loop.

## 6. The Multi-Objective Pareto Frontier

A single weighted score can hide important conflicts. We therefore propose generating a Pareto frontier from simulated and measured candidate configurations.

For each candidate design \(x\), define:

\[
\mathbf{f}(x)=
\begin{bmatrix}
-S(x)\\
E(x)\\
C(x)\\
G(x)\\
R(x)
\end{bmatrix}
\]

where:

- \(S(x)\): delivered service performance, to maximise.
- \(E(x)\): ecological impact, to minimise.
- \(C(x)\): lifecycle cost, to minimise.
- \(G(x)\): lifecycle greenhouse-gas emissions, to minimise.
- \(R(x)\): service risk, including outages and recovery delays, to minimise.

The negative sign converts service maximisation into minimisation.

A design is dominated if another design is at least as good on every objective and strictly better on at least one. The remaining nondominated designs form the Pareto frontier.

The final selection must satisfy hard ecological, safety and legal constraints before commercial preferences are considered.

### Three representative operating strategies

**Ecology-first:** lower compute density, conservative environmental limits and larger safety margins.

**Balanced:** competitive compute capacity, adaptive scheduling, constrained placement and resilient operations.

**Service-first:** higher compute density and utilisation, but only within the same mandatory ecological and safety limits.

These are proposed operating strategies, not experimentally validated outcomes. The frontier must emerge from the model rather than be assumed to follow a particular mathematical curve.

## 7. Service Architecture and Business Model

Antarctic Neocloud would initially target workloads that can tolerate higher or variable network latency.

Potential workload categories include:

- Batch AI training.
- Scheduled inference.
- Large-scale scientific computing.
- Distributed data processing.
- Other delay-tolerant GPU workloads.

Latency-sensitive applications should not be the initial target unless network testing demonstrates a competitive end-to-end path.

### Revenue streams

1. **Compute-as-a-service:** rental of GPU capacity.
2. **Infrastructure services:** specialised compute for research and scientific workloads.
3. **Thermal resource recovery:** potential revenue from useful heat or recovered water, subject to demonstrated economics.
4. **Hydrogen and salt products:** optional future revenue streams requiring separate technical, environmental and legal validation.

### Commercial metrics

The business case would compare the subsea design against equivalent terrestrial infrastructure using:

- Cost per useful GPU-hour.
- Compute utilisation and availability.
- End-to-end latency and bandwidth.
- Power consumption per delivered compute-hour.
- Lifecycle greenhouse-gas emissions.
- Maintenance and recovery costs.
- Environmental exposure per unit of compute.

The platform must outperform or provide a defensible strategic advantage over terrestrial alternatives. Novelty alone is not a business model.

## 8. Research Evidence and Technical Precedents

The concept builds on several established research areas.

### Subsea data-centre feasibility

**Microsoft Project Natick** demonstrated a full-scale subsea data-centre module in the North Sea. Microsoft reported a server failure rate one-eighth that of its land-based control group during the trial. This supports the feasibility of investigating sealed, remotely operated compute infrastructure, but does not establish Antarctic economics or ecological safety.

Source: [Microsoft Research — Project Natick](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/project/natick/)

### Sustainable cooling and lifecycle assessment

Fontoura and colleagues' 2025 *Nature* study, *Using life cycle assessment to drive innovation for sustainable cool clouds*, evaluates cooling technologies across the wider data-centre lifecycle. The study reports potential reductions in greenhouse-gas emissions, energy demand and blue-water consumption for the cooling technologies examined.

Source: [Nature, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08832-3)

### Integrated cooling and desalination

Kanbur and colleagues' 2020 study, *Multi-criteria thermoeconomic and thermodynamic assessments of the desalination-integrated two-phase liquid-immersion data center cooling system*, investigates using data-centre waste heat to drive membrane distillation.

The study provides a direct precedent for analysing thermal integration, costs and multi-objective trade-offs. Its experimental and design scale does not establish commercial Antarctic performance.

Source: [International Journal of Energy Research, 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/er.5677)

### Marine discharge impacts

Roberts and colleagues' 2010 review, *Impacts of desalination plant discharges on the marine environment: A critical review of published studies*, examines evidence concerning salinity, temperature, contaminants and ecological effects. It emphasises the importance of discharge-site selection and adequate monitoring.

Source: [Water Research, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20633919/)

A 2024 review in *Environmental Science & Technology* examines the potential effects of desalination brine on benthic organisms and habitats, reinforcing that avoiding seabed-mounted equipment does not eliminate discharge-related seabed exposure.

Source: [ACS Publications, 2024](https://pubs.acs.org/esthag/article/58/13/5631/154177/Impacts-of-Desalination-Brine-Discharge-on-Benthic)

### Southern Ocean circulation and ecosystems

Rintoul's 2018 *Nature* review, *The global influence of localized dynamics in the Southern Ocean*, explains the importance of Southern Ocean circulation to climate and ocean-system processes.

Source: [Nature, 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0182-3)

These sources establish relevant technical precedents and environmental concerns. They do not demonstrate that the complete Antarctic Neocloud system is commercially viable or environmentally safe. That remains the subject of our proposed validation.

## 9. Prototype and Validation Plan

We propose a simulation-first research prototype before considering any physical deployment.

### Workstream 1: Compute and thermal model

Estimate workload-dependent power draw, coolant temperatures, heat rejection and useful heat recovery.

**Outputs:** compute capacity, thermal balance and energy consumption.

### Workstream 2: Ocean and ecological model

Use oceanographic data to model currents, temperature dispersion and discharge exposure. Incorporate relevant habitat information and uncertainty.

**Outputs:** predicted exposure fields, sensitivity analysis and candidate deployment constraints.

### Workstream 3: Placement and Pareto optimisation

Implement ACO and compare it against baseline placement and alternative optimisation methods.

**Outputs:** candidate configurations, nondominated solutions and performance comparisons.

### Workstream 4: Service and economic model

Estimate networking performance, reliability, maintenance, lifecycle costs and compute revenue.

**Outputs:** cost per useful GPU-hour, service availability and lifecycle comparison against terrestrial alternatives.

### Workstream 5: Integrated evaluation

Evaluate three scenarios:

1. Conventional subsea placement without ecological optimisation.
2. ACO-based placement with ecological risk included in the objective.
3. ACO-based placement with hard ecological constraints and adaptive shutdown policies.

Compare all scenarios using the same workload assumptions, power conditions and service requirements.

The prototype will test whether ecological constraints can be enforced while retaining useful compute capacity. It will not claim to certify real-world ecological safety without field validation.

## 10. Risks and Conditions for Proceeding

The project must explicitly address the following risks.

**Engineering:** mooring fatigue, corrosion, platform stability, power failure, fibre outages and difficult recovery.

**Ecological:** thermal exposure, acoustic disturbance, chemical leakage, artificial habitat effects, brine discharge and cumulative fleet impacts.

**Economic:** high deployment costs, repair logistics, network latency and competition with terrestrial infrastructure.

**Energy:** insufficient power availability, competing cooling and electrolysis loads, and an unfavourable lifecycle emissions balance.

**Legal:** environmental assessment, applicable maritime and Antarctic jurisdiction, protected areas and restrictions on mineral-resource activities.

The Antarctic Environmental Protocol requires environmental impact assessment for activities in its area. It also prohibits mineral-resource activities there, except scientific research. Commercial salt extraction therefore requires specific legal review based on the proposed location and activity.

Source: [Antarctic Treaty Secretariat — Environmental Impact Assessment](https://www.ats.aq/e/eia.html)

The project should not proceed to physical deployment unless its location, energy supply, service economics, environmental limits, recovery plan and legal pathway have been independently evaluated.

## 11. Success Criteria

The venture will be evaluated against measurable criteria rather than broad sustainability claims.

- Demonstrate a complete energy balance for compute, cooling, networking, monitoring and optional process equipment.
- Show whether the proposed placement method improves the quality of feasible solutions compared with alternative algorithms.
- Produce a Pareto frontier using consistent service and ecological metrics.
- Quantify uncertainty in environmental predictions and test adverse operating scenarios.
- Demonstrate a credible recovery and decommissioning strategy.
- Compare lifecycle cost and emissions against equivalent terrestrial compute.
- Establish whether heat recovery creates a net benefit after all additional equipment and operating loads are included.
- Identify a legally and environmentally defensible pathway for any future deployment.

No numerical improvement target will be presented as achieved until the prototype produces reproducible results.

## 12. Conclusion

Antarctic Neocloud proposes a different approach to compute infrastructure: design the compute network, thermal system, ecological monitoring and operational controls as one integrated system.

Its distinctive contribution is not simply underwater cooling. It is the attempt to make ecological limits an enforceable part of infrastructure placement and workload orchestration, while evaluating the full economic and environmental consequences of the deployment.

The research supports several components of this vision, including subsea compute demonstrations, lifecycle-aware cooling and waste-heat integration. The complete concept remains unvalidated, particularly for Antarctic deployment, fleet-scale environmental effects and commercial resource recovery.

Our next step is to build a reproducible simulation that determines whether the integrated design can deliver competitive compute performance within defensible ecological constraints.

**The objective is not to maximise the amount of compute extracted from the ocean. It is to identify the most capable infrastructure configuration that remains environmentally, operationally and economically defensible.**
